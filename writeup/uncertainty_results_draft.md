The receiver example asks whether tracking improves separation prediction after conventional measurables, draft information and NFL context are included. Across 23 evaluation receivers, baseline RMSE was 0.23572 yards versus 0.23577 with TURN. Both point errors round to 0.24 yards, but that does not establish equivalence. The gain interval, [−0.00903, +0.01267] yards, permits both higher and lower prediction error.

These errors compare each athlete's mean separation and prediction over his realised NFL contexts. They are not errors on individual routes or forecasts of future playing roles. Every athlete receives equal scoring weight; receivers have two to 401 valid plays. The prespecified ≥30-play check retains 17 receivers and also has an uncertain gain.

The full nine-comparison record gives the broader context: three point gains are positive and six negative, but none has a marginal interval supporting improvement. The intervals resample evaluation athletes while holding training models fixed, so they omit training-fit uncertainty. Earlier studies already used 2025 outcomes; this is not a pristine holdout. These limits do not prove that tracking is useless, or that more data would necessarily uncover a useful effect. They define the evidence available for these measurements and comparisons.
